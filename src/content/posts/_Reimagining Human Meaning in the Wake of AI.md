# Human Meaning in the Wake of Generative AI

Many have touted the success of large language models (LLMs), citing that they are harbingers of a new era of human creativity---one made effortless through exploitation of machine intelligence. Regardless of whether the bespoke tale of (dare I say supremacist?) human life is realistic at all, the idea of it is appealing for many---it offers endless ease and escape from the burdens of pre-superintelligence (i.e., today's) life. But in reckoning with the immediate possibility that machines will dominate large swaths of human work, one is forced to confront questions about human meaning. 

Nicholas Dames distills the feelings of generational coping with technological revolution into beautiful prose in the Atlantic article [*The Feeling of Becoming Less and Less of a Person*](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/2026/05/ben-lerner-transcription-review/686579/), which is an essay on Ben Lerner’s [*Transcription*](https://bookshop.org/a/12476/9780374618599). He paints the defamiliarization of the self as an almost inescapable process---an erosion of humanity as technology permeates the sensorium in increasingly demanding ways. This defamiliarization penetrates the everyday in unseeable ways. Of course, most people believe this to be true, but it is the beauty and simplicity of Dames' telling of Lerner's novel that makes the notion of technology impeding on humanity feel new. 

But while this feeling of technology ripping away humanity is tacitly held by many, is it true? What necessarily makes technology opposed to human meaning? Of course, the counterargument is that technology is a tool---one that only increases the capicity for human expression. (I must remind readers that this argument sits at the core of the modern selling point for generative AI companies to public consumers). I can think of three classes of struggle which deal with how generative AI specifically may wrestle the feeling of human meaning away from us: (1) the technology detaches us from encountering the experience of everyday life (i.e., outsourcing of cognitive, emotional, physical effort; unrealistic engagement with a social agent), (2) the machine's ability to solve problems makes struggling toward a goal by one's self seem pointless, (3) the technology poses a philosophical and existential question: if AI might mimic and surpass humans in almost every tangible output, what makes human life worth living? These three classes are not completely distinct, but I believe that human experiences with AI often cluster in these ways.


In this article, I will focus on the third class of struggle because I believe it cuts to the root of meaning making in the wake of AI. I also believe it underlies the other two classes. That is, if the question posed in class three were resolved, the issues of classes one and two would no longer be relevant. Now, I have already invoked this term of "human meaning" without defining it. I've gotten into trouble with this term before because I love to discuss meaning---everyone has some intuition about it---but it is very hard to pinpoint a specific definition. In this article, I will refer to two types of meaning and then argue for their interrelation. 

The first type of meaning is simply the subjective feeling of meaning. One should not underestimate subjective feeling as a valid way to define a concept. Subjective feeling does not point to mechanism in any way, but it does point to emergence---feelings (along with every other human behavior) emerges from the physics of a brain-body-environment system, and thus are valid targets for understanding. Feeling that one can derive meaning from life and converselfy, that one's life is meaningful, is a core factor in human wellbeing. Multiple mental health struggles involve a feeling that one's life lacks meaning. If we can understand and regulate how AI impacts human meaning in this way, we will have tangible ways to mitigate the negative effects of AI on mental wellbeing that we see today (i.e., reports of suicide, over-reliance on AI companions, harmful dialogues about mental health (CITATION)).

The second type of meaning relies on the notion of subjectivity. Let some item exist in the universe. This item may have measurable properties---i.e., one may use some instrument to extract objective measurements (though we must consider arguments that no measurement can be purely objective (CITATION)). We then can define subjectivity as properties of an item that go beyond objective measurements. In other words, subjectivity is the product of the interaction between an observer and an item being observed. The making of subjective meaning is a process. It relies on some process intrinsic to an observer and the entering of signals external to the observer that perturb this process. Thus, our second type of meaning is the restructuring of signals from the world in a way that is specific to an observer. 

Before we embark on a scrutiny of these two forms of meanings and how they apply to the human experience of AI, I will call to mind the philosophy of how to seek out meaning as tought by Victor Frankl in his book *A Man's Search for Meaning*. Frankl argues that to find meaning in one's life, we ought to not define a final, stagnant sort of ultimate meaning. There is no ultimate meaning in life, he argues. Meaning differs from person to person and moment to moment. Frankl then states that to find meaning, one has to answer the call of life in that very moment: what does life ask of me now? How do I respond? In this way, meaning is about a responsibility. A responsibility to answer life righteously depending on the specific tasks that it assigns you. In keeping with this idea by Frankl, we should ask ourselves now what the AI turningpoint demands of us. In my mind, it is a meta-reckoning with human meaning itself. The advent of high-performing human intelligences poses a question about meaning that we are all asked to answer. Within the next decade or so, our answers will set the course of humanity on its path for the coming centuries. Because of AI, it is an extremely meaningful time to be alive as a human. Not only for those directly involved with developing and curating the next AI systems, but for every human who is defining their existence in the wake of the technology. If for nothing else, we can thank it for this opportunity to reimagine and reclaim human meaning.

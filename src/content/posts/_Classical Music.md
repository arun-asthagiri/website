### Do you bite your thumb at me, sir?
When I think of a classical music concert, I picture white-haired men with an insufferable tendancy for coughing loudly all of a sudden. This group sits, reserved, as they watch a concert in a cathedral-looking concert hall. A holy place for holy men. The concert unfolds like a museum painting behind glass---otherwordly, untouchable, preserved, framed, and static. At the end of the first movement, the white-haired men happily scorn the newcomer that claps. The scorn is only tradition, a concert ritual if you will, and they enjoy it deeply. Of course, the scorn is all-the-more potent because it is hidden under an expression of snarky disbelief. "How does such a sacriligious novice think they have the right to enjoy such untouchable music?". They revel in the embarassment of the newcomer whose face is now flushed red. At the end of the concert, the white-haired men sign their checks and send them off to the failing orchestra. The director now has a few more years to appease their dying crowd. And so on. A sickly visage of classical music, indeed. 

But it is not the story I want to tell. I want to tell the story of the classical musician. Young and bright-eyed. She deeply feel in love with the craft, the art, the depth and the terror of the work. I want to tell a story of youthful passion. The passion that fueled and warmed her as she slowly faced the frigid cold of her white-haired audience. I want to tell the story of youthful passion against the backdrop of a dying snobbish culture because I think people need to realize that the lust for musical depth is not lost in classical music. It is just masked. Then, I want to explore how the elitist mask of classical music has reshaped what passion means for the brilliant musician. And to give a spoiler, I'll argue that modern classical musicians have rechanneled their passsion from exuberant boldness into bold subtelty. They place their genius in crazy details that the white-haired men can't hear. And so, they live vicariously by laughing at their audiences and crying at the same time at the absurdity of it all.

### Historical aside
To be sure, the passion of young classical musicians is not new. The great talents of every generation have (and must have) sacrificed themselves at the alter of their instruments. So, we have had generations of masters, and yet each generation is so different. Trends in musicality emerge at the generational level in classical music.These trends in musicality are shaped by the environmental pressures of music culture---the audience, the donors, the industry. Understanding the contextual scene of music gives us a lens into why classical music sounds the way it does today. (Not for better, not for worse, just different).

Before we embark on our story, let me present some artifactual data. Consider this recording by Jascha Heifetz. Jascha Heifetz is considered by many to be the greatest violinist of the 20th century. And note, the 20th century of classical music is called its "golden age". And so, Jascha Heifetz was the golden violinist of the golden age. And his playing represented his time and his culture. Jascha Heifetz studied at moved from Russia to the United states at the ripe age of 16 to escape the 1917 Russian Revoultion.

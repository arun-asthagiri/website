# Reflections on Music, Models, and Brains: SMPC and CCN Conferences 2026

I had the privelege of attending two conferences over the last few weeks that were outwardly really different---one was the Society for Music Perception and Cognition (SMPC), the other was Computational Cognitive Neuroscience (CCN)---but they agreed in a pursuit of understanding how the human mind engages with a complex and dynamic world.
    * I'm writing this to reflect on what I learned from these conferences hoping this will be interesting for people who like to geek out about music, the brain, computational modeling, or any combination of these.
        * At SMPC, I presented on how generatve models of music recapitulate some fundamental findings in music perception (like octave equivalence and circular key area relationships) and how they can be used to understand musical predictions in the brain during naturalistic listening.
            * The project applies recent advances in music and machine learning to better understand music perception in humans. While many of the methods are new, the approach follows from a long tradition of using computational models to understand basic components of music perception. 
                * For example, perceptual spaces/expectation models.
                * And this branch of computational modeling in music perception sprouts from a more general branch of modeling in the cognitive sciences.
                    * Computational models have been a methodological backbone of how scientists have studied the mind and brain.
                        * Some classic examples include the question of how humans draw conclusions from pieces of evidence---one way is deductive reasoning to determine what logically follows from certain statements (this lends itself to a symbolic model of formal logic) and another process might be using inductive reasoning to determine what beliefs are likely given our observations (this lends itself to a Bayesian model of probablistic learning).
                        * Another great example is the classic debate between whether humans acquire language by learning the rules versus the statistics that govern speech and text. Chomsky argued that a statistical approach cannot explain some aspects of language (like how we know that certain compositional sentences are gramatically correct: a dog that was about to walk while eating grass while barking while wagging its tail sat down, which can only be verified through long-range interactions between parts of the sentence and is thus not solvable by fixed-order Markovian-based statistical models). Yet, we also know that statistical learning plays a role in how infants acquire language (Saffran).
                        * In each of these cases, psychologists and cognitive scientists theorized about why the mind might give rise to certain kinds of behavior, developed models (either implicitly or explicitly) about the process, and tested predictions from their models using carefully controlled experimental paradigms.
                    * All of this is to say: computational models have proven extremely important in formalizing and testing certain theories about how the brain and mind works^[*an aside on computationalism*]. The questions that arise then are: what kinds of models are useful, when are they useful, and how should they be used?
                    * In the modern age of neuroscience and AI, these questions are especially important to unpack because we now have really complicated models that can achieve human-level performance on some tasks, but are incommensurable with human minds in some fundamental ways ^[*example of the AI-brain gap*].
                        * The CCN 2026 conference centered on this issue of understanding how deep learning models compare with human minds and whether and how they can be useful scientifically.
                        *  some sessions at this conference were titled: 
                            * Does prediction in NeuroAI reveal things about mind and brain?
                            * We can predict the brain using DNNs. Now what?
                    * To set the stage for CCN 2026, we should understand the context for why people are using deep learning models in neuroscience. The motivation comes from a (now huge) number of papers  showing that deep neural networks in vision and language show surprising alignment with the brain.
                        * In fact, these models are better predictors of brain activity than any set of hand-coded models that have previously been used in neuroscience. 
                        * But prediction is not understanding, and this is the central issue in the computational cognitive neuroscience field right now: linking artificial neural networks to a brain may simply be "using black boxes to predict other black boxes". Are there methods here that actually deepen insight into what a brain is doing?

## My takeaways. 

* **What are computational models useful for?**

![The system of interest...](./_assets/system-experiment.jpg)

* Let's zoom out on the process of doing a human-subjects experiment. As a concrete example, let's say we're studying how the brain responds to harmonic violations in music.
    * At a high level, the brain is a system that operates under certain constraints. These constraints include the biology of how neurons fire, the metabolic regulation of the brain and body, the connectivity between regions of the brain, how this connectivity changes as a function of experience, etc.^[one implication of a brain's constraints]
    * When we're designing psychological experiments, we're often interested in characterizing how our system of interest responds to certain types of inputs. Our approach is typically to manipulate a feature of the input and observe how that affects the response. In our example, this might be how a brain responds to chords that are expected versus unexpected under a certain harmonic context.
    * When we're designing a computational model for the system, we're interested in what constraints of the system give rise to the observed input→response behavior. Here, we'd manipulate the ingredients that go into our computational model, and evaluate how well the model fits the human data.
* To make this distinction more concrete, let's consider two classic experiments that study harmonic expectation: [Koelsch et al., 2000](https://doi.org/10.1162/089892900562183) and [Cheung et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067).
    * In Koelsch et al., 2000, the authors vary the input to the system of interest. They present chord sequences that are common in Western classical music (i.e., I->I->IV->V->I) and chord sequences that include a less conventional Neopolitan 6th chord to compare harmonic violations against expected chords. They find a neural component (a right anterior ERP ~150 ms after chord onset) that differentiates expected versus unexpected harmonies.
    * In Cheung et al., 2019, the authors use a computational model of harmonic expectation: they predict the probability of each chord in a sequence based on the previous chords. The model is trained on Western popular music progressions. They find that components derived from the model of expectation (i.e., surprise and uncertainty) align with brain activity in certain regions recorded with fMRI. 
    * What can we conclude from both these approaches?
        * The stimuli manipulation approach (Koelsch) can tell us about how the brain responds to harmonic violations. It does so by looking at a very specific harmonic violation. The limitation is that we can't generalize to chord progressions that normally occur in music because they vary along many more dimensions that the one isolated in the experiment (in-key vs neopolitan 6th). Follow-up studies using a similar approach might eventually characterize what expectation violations look like in the brain for many other harmonic, melodic, rhythmic, and timbral stimuli variations, but this requires separate experimental manipulations for each of these dimensions. 
        * On the other hand, the modeling approach (Cheung) starts by making a hypothesis about what sorts of constraints are necessary for a system to develop harmonic expectations in the first place. These constraints take the form of the design principles behind the computational model: the inductive biases (or architecture), task (what is the model trained to do), data (what is it trained on), and learning rule (how does data change the model).
        * For the Cheung example, this is an N-gram model, predicting the next chord in a sequence, trained on Western chord sequences, and trained to minimize surprisal over the next chord. Now, we can interpret the finding of human-alignment as evidence in favor of these design constraints contributing to harmonic expectations in humans.
            * This is meaningful because we've now formalized specific system constraints (learning and prediction) that give rise to harmonic expectations in humans.
            *  Future experiments can then vary the 4 dimensions of model constraints (inductive bias, task, data, learning rules) to systematically test how they give rise to human-like harmonic expectations.

                       
  * So, computational models can be useful because they allow us to ask questions beyond what variables X produce what response Y in humans, and instead ask: what constraints X give rise to responses $\hat{Y}$ that account for human responses $Y$ [Figure 2. Experimental space...].
      *  The beauty of computational modeling in this way is that we move beyond understanding the effects of inputs on a system and towards the underlying factors that cause the system to respond in the way it does.
          *  Of course, this doesn't mean that computational models tell us about the physical mechanism in a brain that underlies the response at the "implementational" level, to use [Marr's term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Level_of_analysis#Marr's_tri-level_hypothesis). That's why I think the concept of a "constraint" is helpful. In the Cheung example, the constraint that the model must learn transition probabilities between chords is a high-level constraint: the neural firing patterns may not directly code for chord transition probabilities, but this constraint in the computational model may be a good high-level abstraction for what the brain is doing physically.
      * One other affordance of computational models is that they allow us to study humans in more naturalistic settings. This is useful because it allows for generalization that is typically intractable with traditional experiment design.
          * For harmonic expectation, we would have to separately design experiments to test effects of all combinations of chord qualities with different degrees of expectedness. With a computational model, we can quickly scale up to describing behavior for naturally-occuring chord sequences (as Cheung et al., 2019 did).

**Hypothesis testing with computational models:**
* A fancy computational model doesn't let you escape from good old hypothesis testing and careful manipulation. Even if we built a computational model that is a great fit for human behavior, it doesn't mean that we understand that behavior any better. I think advancing scientific understanding through computational models will come from combining the benefits of experimental control with the flexibility of a model. One cool example of this is a recent [study on selective attention](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02414-7) in the "cocktail party" effect. The authors developed a computational model of selective attention and showed that the model replicated multiple "confusion" effects that happen in humans. Then, they made predictions about human behavior using the model finding that there should be differential confusion for horizontal versus vertical sound source displacement. They used their model-derived predictions to design a novel selective attention experiment for humans, and showed that again, human behavior aligned with model predictions. 
    * In this vein, I think there are two tracks of manipulation that tend to be effective when taking a computational modeling approach.
    * Approach 1: manipulate the stimuli. You can alter certain properties of the stimulus, feed those conditions to both the model and to humans, and ask how their responses align and differ. This can tell you whether the tranformations that both apply to the sensory input are similar---and how they align along the specific dimension you manipulated.
    * Approach 2: manipulate the modeling paradigm: XYZ. Perform model comparison to see which model provides the best explanation for the data. This is the same spirit as what we are doing when we test scientific theories, but it comes from a more probabilistic approach. Importantly, I'm talking about models that never "see" human data. This makes them "pure" models in the sense that they are modeling the underlyling hypothesized process rather than being fit directly to a human outcome. 

Manipulating both modeling constraints and experimental stimuli, we can advance our understanding of how a system (i.e., a human brain) interacts with its environment (i.e., music) give rise to certain responses. 

* **Are deep learning models going to be useful for understanding the brain and music?**
    * Deep learning models, and specifically multi-layer artificial neural networks, are a specific class of model that performs a task by transforming an input space into an output space through multiple nonlinear transformations.
        * The flexibility of these models (the fact that they can approximate any mathematical function) allows them to perform well on many difficult tasks including music and text generation.
        * However, the big question is whether they are interpretable enough to advance scientific understanding.
        * If we return to the idea of manipulating model constraints, the architecture of a deep neural network is quite hard to systematically manipulate because there are so many parameters. But the other 3 dimensions---the task, the data, and the learning rule---are the same as simpler modeling paradigms. (i.e., you could replace a large neural network with a linear model and keep the other 3 modeling dimensions the same). In this sense, deep neural networks still remain really useful because we can manipulate these other 3 modeling dimensions and see how this affects alignment with humans.
        * Some people might argue that deep neural networks are not useful for undertanding humans because their architecture is fundamentally different from a brain.
            * I agree. If you are interested in how the architecture of a modern neural network aligns with the biology of a brain, you probably won't get that far because a brain is a fundamentally different system from deep neural networks---the gap ranges from what a "neuron" is to how the network dynamics play out.^[*examples of the AI-brain gap*]
            * But, we can also view the architecture of the neural network as somewhat arbitrary because it is simply a very flexible nonlinear function. In this case, it doesn't matter how that function is implemented, what matters more is how the other constraints (task, data, and learning rule) shape the function that is learned.
                * The reason why it might be valid to not care so much about architecture is because the brain is also a very flexible learner. A great example of this is sensory substitution---people can learns how to interpret signals from modality through another modality (see [this foundational study](https://doi.org/10.1038/221963a0), and [this book](https://books.google.nl/books/about/Livewired.html?id=SkCTEAAAQBAJ&redir_esc=y)). In other words, there is likely very little hardwired cortical architecture dedicated to interpreting a specific modality of sensory input. This is just one type of example of the flexibility of what a brain can learn to implement. Given this flexibility, the focus can turn toward asking how the other constraints shape what the brain learns rather than asking how a specific architecture gives rise to a specific function in the brain.
            * Still, the understanding of how learning-based constraints shape neural networks is itself a really difficult problem and an open question in modern machine learning.
            * The hope is that through the systematic manipulations covered above, we will advance our interpretability of these aritificial neural networks alongside our understanding the human brain. After all, this synergy between understanding human minds and artificial networks was at the heart of how these models were originally developed. Key figures like David Rumelhartd, McClelland, Geoffrey Hinton were all interested in understanding the human mind, and as a result developed computational models that laid the foundation for the modern artificial neural network.
            * So, the short answer is: deep learning models are potentially useful for providing an understanding how constraints shape learning in a really flexible way, though we need more interpretability work to really disentangle how that works in these models. 
  

**Conclusions:**

I think it isn't a coincidence that many of the fundamental theories in neuroscience and cognitive science lend themselves well to computational models: e.g., predictive processing, reinforcement/reward learning, associative conditioning, Hebbian learning. The field of music perception and cognition has and will benefit from computational modeling because it allows us to test how contraints rather than features of a musical input give rise to behavior. That's particularly useful for the study of music because the human response to music is so varied---within and across individuals. There is no recipe for how a song gives rise to a subjective experience. Instead, it is generaly more helpful to ask how the biological system interacts with the song to produce experience. A key aspect of that human system is how we learn from musical experience, which lends itself really well to models that learn from the statistics of an environment. 

Modeling also allows our field to turn toward more naturalistic and diverse music, which increases generalizability. This has been a key limitation of previous studies that focus on symbolic components of Western art music. The flexibility of some newer models is that they can be trained on musical recordings rather than symbolic representations, opening doors for understanding non-notatable components of music. 

Finally, I believe that music perception is a really interesting area for studying how the human mind differs from machines. That question belonged to sci-fi a few years ago. Now, it's becoming increasingly important to ask how humans make sense of the world differently from artificial intelligence models. Music is a great testbed for this because the subjective experience of music and the act of making music are some of the things that us humans identify as "deeply human". People who study music perception and cognition already have an arsenal of tools for understanding the things that make music "deeply human"---there have been amazing cross-cultural studies of music, cross-species studies, the study of musical pleasure and affect, the study of enculturation and learning. By combining the traditional experimental approach in music perception and cognition with modern computational tools, I think we can go even deeper into understanding what qualities (and constraints) of the human-music interaction give rise to such a meaningful experience. 



[*an aside on computationalism*]: Using a computational model to understand a brain or mind does not mean that you assume the brain or mind to be computational. I like to think of computation as a useful metaphor for what a brain might be doing at a certain level of abstraction. The question of whether human biology actually performs computations requires defining what computation actually is. One might recall Alan Turing's approach, which is to define computation as an algorithm applied to a set of symbols that can be implemented with read, write, and memory steps. Colloquially, a process is computational if we can perform it by following a set of rules using a pen and a sheet of paper. In this sense, the brain may or may not be computational (it would require dynamically stable and recurrent states over which some controller operates algorithmically) and this is an open question in theoretical neuroscience. It's also interesting to ask whether the property "computational" is determined by the dynamical system we are studying (i.e., a brain) or arises from the interaction between an experimenter (that provides a certain ontological perspective like "the brain is computational") and the system of interest. [one implication of a brain's constraints]: One constraint (or design property) of a brain is that it is recurrent, meaning that the brain's state at a given moment in time feeds back into its state at the next moment in time. A key implication is that brain dynamics are determined not only by the input, but also by the brain state before the input arrives. In other words a brain's response to a stimulus $A$ might look very different at time $t$ versus time $t+n$. This observation poses an issue for traditional psychological experiments, which typically assume time-invariant responses to stimuli (that the stimulus $A$ has the same effect at time $t$ and time $t+n$ where recorded differences are only due to measurement noise). It might be the case that if you are not interested in a time-variant property of a brain, this assumption is okay because averaging responses over many trials can reduce the impact of the time-varying components while emphasizing the time-invariant components of the effect---this is the approach for classical ERP studies. However, this assumption is not met if the time-variant components of the response compound with averaging rather than averaging out to 0---an example of this is if the context for a stimulus completely changes the direction of the effect. This is actually what expectation is---a change in the effect of a stimulus (sound onset) due to its preceeding context. That means that in studies of musical expectation, the time-variant and recurrent properties of the brain are directly responsible for the effect of interest.
